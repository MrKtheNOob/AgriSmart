# Crop Explanation Knowledge Base for AgriSmart

## Scope and usage boundaries

This knowledge base provides detailed agronomic context for ten Senegalese vegetable crops. It is intended to explain why each crop might be suitable in a given location, what vulnerabilities remain, and how to manage them – **not** to rank crops or give suitability scores. All information is evidence-based with a focus on Senegal-specific and West African research. When evidence from other regions is used, this is explicitly noted. We do not rely on generic cultivation guides or personal advice; every claim is linked to a credible source. Cultivar differences, conflicting reports, and gaps in Senegalese data are highlighted under **Evidence limitations** for each crop.

## Tomato

### Identity and production context

Tomato (*Solanum lycopersicum* L.; syn. *Lycopersicon lycopersicum*) – locally often called **tomate** – is a major vegetable in Senegal. It is cited as the **second-most important vegetable crop in Senegal**. Varieties range from local types to improved hybrids (e.g. “Lady Nema”, “Mongal”) selected for heat or salinity tolerance. Tomatoes are widely grown in Senegal’s **Niayes horticultural zone** (Dakar-Thiès-Louga-Saint-Louis region) under irrigated conditions, as well as in river valleys (Senegal River) and some Sahelian oases. Both **open-field and protected/irrigated systems** exist; protected (shade-net) greenhouses are emerging but remain limited. Rainfed tomato is risky in the Sahel due to high evapotranspiration and irregular rainfall; most production relies on supplemental irrigation.

### Soil interpretation

- **Texture and drainage:** Tomatoes prefer loamy to sandy-loam soils with good drainage. Heavy clay or poorly drained soils can cause root rot and reduce fruit set. Well-drained soils prevent waterlogging, which greatly stresses tomato roots.
- **Soil pH:** Optimal pH is roughly 6.0–6.8. Acidic soils (pH <5.5) induce iron and manganese toxicities and can limit yield; strongly alkaline soils (pH >7.5) can induce micronutrient deficiencies. Senegalese soils vary from acidic (south) to neutral (north); severe pH extremes reduce fruit quality and ripening.
- **Organic matter:** Moderate organic carbon improves structure and water-holding capacity. In Niayes, producers use compost and manure to boost fertility (often from livestock effluents). Low organic matter reduces nutrient availability and drought resilience.
- **N, P, K:** Tomatoes are high-demand for N and K (for foliage and fruit quality) and also P (for root development). Deficiencies cause poor growth, delayed flowering and low fruit yield. Inorganic fertilizer or compost is often needed in depleted tropical soils. Excessive N with low P/K can lead to lush foliage but weak fruiting.
- **CEC:** A higher CEC soil (more clay/OM) can hold nutrients and moisture, benefiting tomatoes during dry spells. Sandy soils (low CEC) need more frequent fertilization and irrigation to prevent leaching of N and K.
- **Salinity & waterlogging:** Tomato is **sensitive to soil salinity**. Coastal and river-delta areas of Senegal (Casamance, Gambia, Sine Saloum, Senegal River Delta) suffer from salinization, which significantly reduces tomato yields. Salt stress causes blossom drop and stunted growth. Choosing salt-tolerant varieties (e.g. Lady Nema, Mongal) is recommended for salty soils. Waterlogged soils lead to root hypoxia and increase vulnerability to diseases (e.g. root rot, bacterial wilt). Adequate drainage (e.g. raised beds) is critical in low-lying fields.

### Climate interpretation

- **Temperature sensitivity:** Tomato is a warm-season crop with optimal daytime temperature ~20–25 °C. Temperatures below ~12 °C severely slow growth and can cause blossom drop; above ~35 °C also reduces fruit set. High night temperatures (above ~20–22 °C) accelerate respiration and can reduce fruit retention and sugar content.
- **Heat stress:** Prolonged heat (>35°C) in Senegal’s dry season causes flower abortion and sunscald on fruits, reducing marketable yield. In hot years, yields drop unless heat-tolerant hybrids are used. Evening cooling in Niayes (near the coast) mitigates stress somewhat.
- **Cold stress:** Cold is generally not an issue in Senegal’s lowlands. Only at cooler nights (e.g. December near the ocean) might very young seedlings slow. Transplanting of seedlings is timed after the coldest period.
- **Humidity and disease:** High humidity in rainy season fosters fungal diseases (e.g. early blight *Alternaria* sp., late blight *Phytophthora* sp.) and bacterial speck. Senegal’s relatively dry climate reduces these except in irrigated, dense plantings or the humid south. Mulching or raised beds help dry root zone.
- **Rainfall/water-stress:** Tomatoes need consistent moisture especially from flowering through fruiting. Rainfall in Senegal is seasonal; lack of rain in the dry season necessitates irrigation. Drought stress during flowering causes flowers and immature fruits to drop. Heavy rains may dilute soil nutrients or cause nitrogen leaching.
- **Vulnerable stages:** Flowering and fruit set are most heat-sensitive. Seedling establishment requires moderate warmth (16–30°C). During vegetative growth, water stress delays canopy development. Ripening fruits under stress can become mealy or lack color development.
  
### Water-management implications

- **Soil type:** On light sandy soils, tomato roots penetrate easily but soil holds little water, so frequent irrigation is needed. On heavier loams, soil holds moisture longer but drainage must be assured. Tomato on sandy soils has higher drought and nutrient-leaching risk.
- **Drainage:** Fields must avoid stagnation. Raised beds or mounded rows are common to improve drainage. Overwatering in heavy soils causes root rot; irrigation systems should be well-drained.
- **Critical stages:** Critical water periods are during flowering and early fruit development. Deficit irrigation during vegetative stages can be tolerated, but water stress at bloom causes yield losses. Near harvest, excessive water can swell fruits but may dilute sugars.
- **Irregular rainfall:** Given variable rains, supplemental irrigation should be timed for flowering and fruit fill. Irrigation scheduling (e.g. soil moisture monitoring) is crucial. Rainfall cannot be assumed reliable; farmers often rely on canals or wells in Niayes.
- **Irrigation vs. rainfall:** Rainfall alone (300–400 mm/year in Niayes) is insufficient for tomato yield. Typically, irrigation water (10–20 mm/week) is needed during the dry season. Total seasonal water demand may exceed local rainfall by several times.
- **Drainage & waterlogging:** Fields must avoid pooling; waterlogging can be fatal at any stage. Scheduling irrigation with drying intervals helps. In lighter soils, pulse irrigation can maintain moisture; in heavier soils, avoid continuous flooding.
  
### Principal risks and mitigations

- **Salinity:** High salt in irrigation or soil (coastal intrusion, saline groundwater) causes flower drop and low yields. *Mitigation:* Use salt-tolerant varieties (e.g. Lady Nema, Mongal) and leach soils before planting. Ensure irrigation water is low-salt. 
- **Heat stress:** High daytime/night temperatures reduce fruit set. *Mitigation:* Use heat-tolerant hybrids, provide shade nets or mulch, and irrigate deeply before heat waves to keep plants cool. Harvest early-maturing varieties to avoid peak heat.
- **Drought:** Without irrigation, tomato often fails in Senegal’s dry season. *Mitigation:* Secure irrigation (drip or furrow). Mulch to conserve soil moisture. Prioritize planting in seasons when water is available.
- **Waterlogging:** Heavy rain or irrigation excess causes root diseases. *Mitigation:* Use raised beds and good field drainage. Avoid heavy watering in dense plantings.
- **Nutrient limitation:** Tropical soils often lack N-P-K. *Mitigation:* Apply balanced fertilization (including P and K) and organic amendments. Test soil and tailor fertilizer to local soil fertility (avoid generic rates).
- **Pests (Niayes):** The tomato fruitworm (*Helicoverpa armigera*) is widespread (present in ~92% of Niayes tomato fields, damaging up to 38% of fruits). The invasive tomato leafminer (*Tuta absoluta*) now occurs (~53% fields, especially south Niayes late dry season). Aphids, whiteflies and thrips also attack. *Mitigation:* Employ integrated pest management (IPM): pheromone traps for *Tuta*, biological controls (parasitoids), and targeted pesticides only when thresholds are exceeded. Rotate insecticide modes of action to delay resistance (H. armigera is known for developing resistance).
- **Diseases:** Bacterial wilt and Fusarium root rot can occur in hot, stagnant soils. Early blight risk in humid conditions is low but present if rains occur. *Mitigation:* Use clean transplants, rotate crops, and remove infected plants. Avoid overhead irrigation to reduce foliar disease.
- **Humid-disease pressure:** In greenhouse/high-tunnel systems, downy mildew or damping-off (fungal) can occur. *Mitigation:* Ensure good ventilation, avoid over-watering, and use well-drained media.
  
### Evidence limitations

- **Senegal-specific:** Salt stress information (e.g. Lady Nema variety trials) is Senegalese. Pest surveys (H. armigera, T. absoluta) are Niayes-specific. Climate and cropping information largely from West African dryland systems, which may differ from Senegal’s coastal/horticultural microclimates.
- **Regional/international:** Many tomato guidelines (e.g. exact temperature thresholds, nutrient needs) come from global sources. These may vary by cultivar. For example, some heat-resistant hybrids are sold commercially (hazera sources, etc.) but field performance in Senegal needs local validation.
- **Cultivar differences:** Varieties differ in heat/salinity tolerance and growth habit (determinate vs. indeterminate). Small-fruited types (cherry) need less water than large-fruited. Such differences are often not detailed in sources.
- **Conflicting data:** The attached knowledge base (agronomy_data.md) may contain numeric thresholds; we avoid unverified numbers. Our sources generally do not give exact bounds (e.g. “optimal 24°C”), so small numerical disagreements are not resolved here.
- **Missing data:** There is limited published research on Senegal’s onion and pepper, and on minor crops like eggplant and watermelon within country. We used regional African data where Senegal-specific studies were absent, but local trials may differ.
- **General vs. local:** For example, rainfall requirements of watermelon or onion are given in textbooks; local evapotranspiration and irrigation methods (drip vs. flood) will change actual needs. We note this but cannot specify Senegal’s exact water budgets without field data.

## Onion

### Identity and production context

Onion (*Allium cepa* L.) is a bulb vegetable locally known as **oignon**. Senegal is a significant onion producer in West Africa. The Niayes region is the main onion belt (including the HortiCôte projects). Onions are typically grown as **rainfed crops in the cooler, wetter season and under irrigation in dry times**. Varieties include local short-day types and improved long-day cultivars suited to Senegal’s climate. There is a tradition of both seed-propagated bulb onions and set/shallot types, depending on microclimate. Farmers often intercrop onions or rotate them with other vegetables. Protected cultivation is rare for onions; most are field-grown.

### Soil interpretation

- **Texture and drainage:** Onions favor loose, well-aerated soils (sandy loam) that allow roots and bulbs to expand. Very heavy clay impedes bulb growth and can cause misshapen bulbs. In Niayes, well-worked soils are common; compacted land would limit yield.
- **Soil pH:** Optimal pH is neutral (6.0–7.0). Senegalese onions generally prefer soils around 6.5. On very acidic soils (<5.5), nutrient uptake (especially phosphorus) drops and bulbs are smaller. Slight acidity (pH≥5.0) can be tolerated if limestone or dolomite is applied.
- **Organic matter:** Onions have shallow roots and limited uptake; adding organic matter (compost, manure) is beneficial to improve water retention and supply nutrients. Low organic C in sandy soils means more frequent N applications are needed.
- **N, P, K:** Onions require consistent but moderate N for leaf growth, P for root/bulb initiation, and potassium for bulb quality. Too much early N delays bulbing and reduces storage life; balanced fertilizer (with attention to phosphorus fixation in tropical soils) is important. Deficiencies cause stunted plants and small bulbs.
- **CEC:** Soils with higher CEC retain nutrients better, reducing leaching. Onions on sandy Niayes soils (low CEC) are susceptible to nutrient runoff, so split fertilizer applications are advised.
- **Salinity & waterlogging:** Onions are sensitive to salinity (especially when irrigating with brackish water) – salt leads to burnt tips and bulb abortion. They also dislike waterlogged soils; standing water can kill bulbs or promote fungal rot (e.g. *Pythium*). Therefore good drainage is critical; raised beds are often used.

### Climate interpretation

- **Temperature sensitivity:** Onions prefer mild conditions (day ~20–25°C, night ~15°C). High temperatures accelerate plant maturity but can cause bolting (premature seed stalks). In Senegal’s hot climate, onions are usually grown in the cooler months or with drip irrigation to cool the root zone.
- **Heat stress:** Prolonged heat (>30°C) during bulbing can induce bolting or split bulbs, reducing quality. Heat also increases transpiration, leading to potential water stress if not irrigated. On the other hand, onions require some heat for bulb formation; very cool nights (<10°C) can delay growth.
- **Cold stress:** Unlikely in Senegal except possibly in February nights in Niayes (rarely below 12°C). A brief cool period can promote uniform bulbing in long-day onion types.
- **Humidity and disease:** High relative humidity favors fungal diseases (downy mildew, purple blotch). Senegal’s dry season climate generally limits these, but irrigation under plastic tunnels can create humid pockets. Good air circulation in plantings is needed.
- **Rainfall/water-stress:** Onions have high water needs through leaf extension and bulb swelling. In rainfed systems, inadequate rains during bulbing cause small, fibrous bulbs. Conversely, too much rain (>700 mm seasonally) can promote rot and dilute bulb sugars. Farmers aim to grow onions in a distinct dry season to reduce disease.
- **Vulnerable stages:** Seedling and bulb initiation stages need adequate moisture; deficits here drastically cut yield. From bulbing to harvest, even soil moisture produces better size and uniform shape.
  
### Water-management implications

- **Soil type:** On sandy soils, irrigation must be frequent because they dry quickly; soil cracks can damage shallow roots. On loams, less frequent but deeper irrigation suffices. Both cases require irrigation planning.
- **Drainage:** Avoid pooling water which causes rot. Fields are often flat with furrow irrigation; waterlogging kills onions quickly. Ensure even field slope or drainage canals.
- **Critical stages:** Bulb initiation and enlargement are most water-sensitive. Drought during these leads to hollow or undersized bulbs. Overirrigation post-bulbing can burst skins and reduce storage life.
- **Irregular rainfall:** Erratic rains at the start of season can impede planting; too much rain at harvest delays harvest and lowers quality. Irrigation scheduling helps compensate for rain gaps or excesses (e.g. covering fields to avoid heavy rain impact near harvest).
- **Rainfall vs. irrigation:** Onions typically need 350–550 mm of seasonal water (including rain). In Niayes, supplemental irrigation (drip or pivot) provides this reliably. Without irrigation, yields collapse due to Senegal’s dry climate outside the short rainy season.

### Principal risks and mitigations

- **Thrips:** The onion thrips (*Thrips tabaci*) is a major pest in hot, dry climates. Thrips populations explode under heat (common in Senegal) and cause scarring on bulbs and leaf damage. *Mitigation:* Use drip irrigation to create more humid leaf conditions (thrips dislike moisture); select thrips-tolerant varieties; apply biological controls (predatory mites) or minimal-risk insecticides when needed.
- **Nutrient limitations:** Low soil fertility is common in Senegal. *Mitigation:* Conduct soil tests; apply fertilizers (particularly P and K) at planting. Organic mulch/compost can supply nutrients and conserve moisture (as used by Niayes vegetable producers).
- **Water stress:** Insufficient irrigation during bulb fill leads to reduced bulb size. *Mitigation:* Ensure reliable irrigation (e.g. use drip systems). Mulch to reduce evaporation.  
- **Excess moisture:** Waterlogging or heavy rains can cause bulb rot. *Mitigation:* Use raised beds and ensure fields drain fully between irrigations. Harvest promptly when the canopy dies back.
- **Disease (purple blotch, downy mildew):** Rare in Senegal’s dry season, but possibly introduced via irrigation. *Mitigation:* Rotate crops out of onions if disease appears. Use disease-free seed and avoid overhead watering.
- **Salinity:** Especially in coastal Niayes, salt intrusion can threaten onions. *Mitigation:* Leach saline soils before planting; use desalinated water or mix with fresh water. Plant salt-tolerant sets if available (onion varieties differ in tolerance).
- **Post-harvest (bulb quality):** Bulbs with high moisture or injury rot in storage. *Mitigation:* Cure bulbs by drying leaves; handle gently to avoid bruises.

### Evidence limitations

- **Senegal-specific:** PROTA notes Senegal among West African onion producers, but detailed local practices (e.g. optimal planting dates in Dakar vs. Niayes) are not well documented. Pest/disease incidence data in Senegal is scarce.
- **Regional/international:** Much onion literature is temperate or from Asia. We infer requirements (water 350–550 mm) but such values vary with climate and genotype.  
- **Cultivar differences:** Onions have day-length requirements. Varieties for tropical environments differ from temperate ones, affecting bulbing. Local long-day varieties may bolt if nights are too short (November–Jan).
- **Conflicting evidence:** Some sources (like Yara guides) allow onion pH down to 4 in organic soils, but most agronomists prefer neutral. We take the middle road.
- **Missing data:** Senegalese trials of irrigation regimes or fertilizer rates for onion were not found. West African data (e.g. Mali studies) may not fully match Senegal’s Niayes conditions.

## Chili Pepper

### Identity and production context

Chili pepper (hot pepper; *Capsicum annuum* L., including *C. chinense* local “piment forts”) provides a spicy condiment widely used in Senegalese cuisine. In West Africa, aromatic hot peppers (*C. chinense*, e.g. Scotch bonnet types) play an important culinary and economic role. Chilies are grown in gardens across Senegal, often intercropped with other vegetables. They are mostly open-field and irrigated (in Niayes) or rainfed in favorable climates. No major protected/greenhouse systems are typical. Varieties range from local landraces to improved F₁ hybrids for yield and disease resistance.

### Soil interpretation

- **Texture and drainage:** Chilis grow well on **loamy, well-drained soils**. Heavy clay can slow root growth and exacerbate rots; coarse sand can cause poor nutrient retention. Generally, a medium-textured soil balances drainage and fertility. Senegal’s sandy-loams in Niayes suit chili well, but require good soil organic amendments.
- **Soil pH:** Optimal pH is about 5.5–7.0 for chilis. Acidic soils (<5.5) limit P availability; alkali soils (>7.5) induce micronutrient deficiencies. Slightly acidic (pH 6–6.5) tends to maximize yields.
- **Organic matter:** Chilis benefit from moderate organic matter, improving moisture and nutrient supply. In very fertile soils, they grow vigorously; in depleted soils (common in Sahel), yields suffer. Adding compost or well-rotted manure enhances flowering and fruit set.
- **N, P, K:** Peppers need balanced fertilization. High nitrogen early supports foliage, but excessive N late can delay fruiting. Adequate phosphorus at planting promotes root development and flowering; potassium improves fruit quality (size, shelf-life). Deficiencies lead to few fruits or deformed pods.
- **CEC:** Soils with higher clay/OM hold more nutrients and water, which can buffer dry spells. Sandy soils (low CEC) require frequent fertilization and irrigation to prevent leaching of N/K.
- **Salinity & waterlogging:** Chili pepper is moderately sensitive to salinity. Saline irrigation water can cause leaf burn and reduced fruit set. Waterlogged soil leads to root oxygen deprivation and increased disease; plants under long waterlogging quickly wilt and die. Good drainage is necessary for field-grown pepper.

### Climate interpretation

- **Temperature sensitivity:** Chili peppers are **heat-loving tropical plants**. They germinate best at 24–30 °C and grow vigorously up to ~30–32 °C. Night temperatures below ~15 °C slow maturation. Very high temperatures (above 35–38 °C) and intense light, however, can reduce fruit set (blossom drop) and cause sunscald.
- **Heat stress:** Hot daytime temperatures in Senegal’s dry season generally favor pepper growth, but extreme heat (>35 °C) may cause curled leaves and fewer fruits. Fruit development is optimal around 28–30 °C; hotter nights reduce pollination success. In a hot pulse, fruit set can collapse, requiring shade or planting along irrigation canals for cooling.
- **Cold stress:** Uncommon in Senegal except marginally near the coast in Dec–Jan. Frost is non-issue. A few cool nights (14–16°C) may delay flowering but typically warm quickly by day.
- **Humidity and disease:** High humidity (as in greenhouse or rainy season) can promote foliar fungal diseases (anthracnose, mildew). Senegal’s general climate is dry, reducing such risks. However, in Niger Basin valleys, occasional high humidity may invite downy or powdery mildew.
- **Rainfall/water-stress:** Chilies need consistent moisture during fruiting. Drought at flowering causes blossom drop; drought during pod fill leads to small, pithy fruits. Conversely, heavy rains can cause root disease and dilute fruit flavor. Ideally, moderate irrigation (30–40 mm/week) supports good yield without excess growth of foliage over fruit.
- **Vulnerable stages:** Flowering is sensitive to extreme heat and drought. Young fruit (1–3 cm) is sensitive to desiccation. Ripening fruits are less sensitive but will not expand if water-starved.

### Water-management implications

- **Soil type:** On sandy soils, peppers must be irrigated more often to maintain moisture. Mulch conserves water and keeps soil warm. On heavier soils, avoid over-irrigation to prevent root rot.
- **Drainage:** Ensure soil does not stay saturated. Pepper roots need aerobic conditions. Mulch can help prevent surface crusting and waterlogging.
- **Critical stages:** During flowering and early fruit set, soil moisture should be high. As fruits mature, slightly reducing water can concentrate sugars, improving pungency. 
- **Irregular rainfall:** Unreliable rains make drip or sprinkler irrigation necessary for stable yields. Incorporate rainwater harvesting if possible. Mulch or cover cropping can moderate soil moisture fluctuations.
- **Irrigation availability:** Many peppers are grown under small-scale drip or furrow irrigation in Niayes. Where irrigation is absent, yields are small or erratic unless the crop is timed exactly for the brief rainy season.

### Principal risks and mitigations

- **Heat/drought:** In extreme heat or lack of water, peppers abort flowers and early fruits, sharply cutting yield. *Mitigation:* Use drought-tolerant hybrids, irrigate regularly, and apply mulch to keep rootzone cool. Plant in seasons with some rain or humidity if possible.
- **High humidity/disease:** If rains overlap with fruiting, anthracnose (*Colletotrichum*), bacterial spot, and fungal rots can occur. *Mitigation:* Avoid overhead irrigation after flowering; ensure air flow. Use drip irrigation and pick fruit promptly to reduce disease exposure.
- **Nutrient deficiency:** In poor soils, peppers may flower poorly. *Mitigation:* Balanced fertilization with emphasis on P and K; use compost to build soil fertility.
- **Insect pests:** Aphids, thrips, whiteflies and mites thrive in pepper fields. They can spread viral diseases (e.g. mosaic viruses). *Mitigation:* Encourage natural enemies; use insect netting or regular scouting. Apply targeted insecticides or biopesticides only as needed.
- **Frost or cold (unlikely):** If a sudden cold snap happens (e.g. rare North-Senegal chilly night), young plants can be stunted. *Mitigation:* Not usually needed; cover plants in extreme event.
- **Wind and sandblasting:** Strong Harmattan winds can sandblast young pepper foliage in the dry season. *Mitigation:* Windbreaks or shading nets can protect young plants.
- **Post-harvest (pungency loss):** Excessive moisture during storage causes fruit shriveling and loss of pungency. *Mitigation:* Dry peppers thoroughly and store in ventilated shade.

### Evidence limitations

- **Senegal-specific:** We lack fine-grained studies on local pepper irrigation schedules or cultivar performance. Cultural importance of local *C. chinense* types is noted in West Africa but agronomy (e.g. heat tolerance of those varieties) is not well documented in Senegal.
- **Regional/international:** Much of our climate and soil guidance is drawn from broader tropical pepper literature. For example, optimal temperatures (~25–30°C) and bolting thresholds are global knowledge, not Senegal trials.
- **Cultivar differences:** Sweet bell peppers (non-pungent *C. annuum*) and hot chilis (*C. chinense*, *C. frutescens*) respond differently to heat (bell peppers are typically more sensitive). Specific data for these differences in Senegal is lacking.
- **Conflicting data:** Local sources may underreport yields due to pests (e.g. yield/ha variation is large regionally). Official data (e.g. FAOSTAT) often lumps hot and sweet peppers together, obscuring type-specific trends.
- **Missing data:** There is scant official information on pepper water needs or N/P/K use in Sahelian environments. International guidance (e.g. USDA extension) may not apply directly.

## Okra

### Identity and production context

Okra (*Abelmoschus esculentus* L. Moench) is a heat-tolerant, short-cycle vegetable grown for its edible pods (called “okra” or *gombo* in French). It is commonly grown across Senegal, often in rainfed gardens or small irrigated plots. Okra thrives in warm, tropical conditions and is grown both by smallholders (intercropped with cereals or cowpeas) and horticultural farmers in Niayes. Varieties include tall (immediate) types and dwarf, bush varieties for intensive production. Okra is versatile: both the pods and young leaves are eaten.

### Soil interpretation

- **Texture and drainage:** Okra tolerates a range of soils but performs best on deep, well-drained loams. It has a sturdy taproot, so it can handle moderately heavy soils better than carrot or lettuce. However, poorly drained or compacted soils cause root stress and favor fungal diseases. Sandy soils allow easy root penetration but require more moisture management.
- **Soil pH:** Optimal pH is ~6.0–7.5. Okra will grow in slightly acidic soils (~5.5) but nutrient uptake (especially P and K) suffers. In alkaline soils, micronutrient deficiencies (iron, zinc) can appear. In Senegal’s soils, near-neutral pH in Niayes and northern zones suit okra.
- **Organic matter:** Okra responds to added organic matter. High OM improves pod yield and plant vigor. Low-fertility soils (common in Sahel) yield fewer pods. Incorporating compost or legume residues at planting greatly benefits okra stands.
- **N, P, K:** Moderate N encourages vegetative growth and pod production; too much N can lead to excessive leafiness at the expense of pods. Phosphorus is important for root development and early flowering; potassium improves stress tolerance and fiber quality of pods. Nutrient-poor soils should be fertilized, as okra is often grown continuously in gardens.
- **CEC:** Higher CEC soils buffer nutrient supply and retain moisture, which helps okra during dry spells. Low CEC sandy soils require more frequent fertilization.
- **Salinity & waterlogging:** Okra is relatively **salinity tolerant** compared to many vegetables, but extreme salt still reduces pod yield and quality. Waterlogged soils, however, are harmful: okra’s roots need oxygen to support fast vegetative growth and flowering. Standing water quickly stresses plants, leading to stunted growth or rot.

### Climate interpretation

- **Temperature sensitivity:** Okra is a **warm-season crop** par excellence. Seeds germinate well at 20–35 °C and plants grow vigorously in 25–35 °C. It tolerates very high temperatures that many vegetables cannot. Cool nights (<15 °C) slow growth and can delay flowering.
- **Heat stress:** Okra thrives in heat, but extreme heat (above ~40 °C) can scorch leaves and cause some fruit shedding. Generally, high Senegalese temperatures from February to May are suitable for okra growth (and limit competition from cooler-season crops).
- **Cold stress:** Okra is very frost-sensitive (not relevant to Senegal’s winter lows) and even a few nights near 10 °C will slow or halt growth. Farmers do not plant okra during the coolest months.
- **Humidity and disease:** High humidity can encourage fungal diseases (powdery mildew on leaves), but Senegal’s generally dry, hot climate reduces incidence. However, if okra is grown where irrigation or rain leaves foliage wet overnight, powdery mildew can appear, stunting plants.
- **Rainfall/water-stress:** Okra is moderately drought-tolerant once established (due to deep root), but severe or prolonged drought will reduce pod number and size. Conversely, excessive rain, especially during harvest, makes pods turn fibrous or split. Timely harvesting avoids losses in rainy periods.
- **Vulnerable stages:** Flower buds and very young pods can abort if the plant is moisture-stressed. The vegetative phase (leaf and branch growth) tolerates some drought. Seedling establishment needs adequate moisture; otherwise stand counts drop significantly.

### Water-management implications

- **Soil type:** In sandy soils, okra’s deep taproot is advantageous, but irrigation or rainfall must reach at least 30–40 cm to sustain plants. On heavy soils, surface moisture lasts longer, but the risk of root rot (e.g. *Phytophthora* damping-off) increases.
- **Drainage:** Ensure fields drain within days after heavy rains. Okra can tolerate occasional wet feet but not standing water for more than a day. Drainage furrows help.
- **Critical stages:** Flowering to pod fill requires consistent moisture for best yields. Stress at flowering causes flowers and young fruits to drop.
- **Irregular rainfall:** As a resilient crop, okra can buffer short droughts; however, prolonged dry spells (weeks without water) lower yields significantly. Supplemental irrigation during dry spells can stabilize yields, though many small farmers rely solely on rain.
- **Irrigation availability:** Where available, light irrigation every 10–14 days keeps okra productive. Drip irrigation works well (no fruit rot from water on leaves). Rainfed okra can succeed with Senegal’s rains (700–1000 mm/yr), but uniform distribution is needed.

### Principal risks and mitigations

- **Drought:** Despite some tolerance, extended drought shrinks okra pods and reduces harvest frequency. *Mitigation:* Use drought-tolerant cultivars, mulch, and supplemental irrigation (if available). Stagger planting dates so not all plants mature in peak drought.
- **Excess moisture:** Waterlogging or heavy rains during harvest make pods tough and low-quality. *Mitigation:* Ensure field drainage; avoid late-season planting that would mature in rainy season. Harvest pods promptly to avoid rot after rains.
- **Nutrient deficiency:** In poor soils, okra yields poorly (thin stalks, few pods). *Mitigation:* Rotate with legumes (e.g. cowpea) or apply compost/manure to rebuild fertility before planting okra.
- **Pests:** Common pests include aphids, whiteflies, and the okra jassid (leafhopper), which suck sap and transmit diseases. Caterpillars (maruca pod borer) can chew pods. *Mitigation:* Intercropping with cereals sometimes reduces outbreaks. Natural enemies (e.g. ladybugs, predatory wasps) help; reserve insecticides for heavy infestations.
- **Diseases:** Powdery mildew (*Erysiphe cichoracearum*) can appear in humid weather, causing white leaf patches. *Mitigation:* Use resistant varieties if known; space plants to improve airflow. Remove infected debris.
- **Heat (excessive):** While tolerant, if midday heat is extreme (>40°C), partial leaf scorch or wilting can occur. *Mitigation:* Provide mulching or shade (in intensive plots) to reduce stress, and ensure roots are well hydrated.
- **Post-harvest:** Okra is highly perishable (pods spoil in 2–4 days at 25–30°C) with sugary slime. *Mitigation:* Harvest pods when young (5–8 cm), handle gently to avoid bruising, and cool after harvest if possible (even a simple shaded box).

### Evidence limitations

- **Senegal-specific:** Little formal research on okra in Senegal. General statements on pests/diseases come from broader African agriculture sources (e.g. Ghana, Nigeria), and may not reflect local pest pressure exactly.
- **Regional/international:** Many okra guidelines (e.g. “thrips and mites common in okra”) are from West African reviews. We expect these apply but local incidence could vary. 
- **Cultivar differences:** Okra has many landraces. Some are very drought-hardy, others not. Senegalese farmers select locally adapted types; agronomic data by variety is lacking.
- **Conflicting data:** Research sometimes conflicts on okra’s exact water needs. We caution that 700–1000 mm annual rainfall (West Africa seed melons) does not equal okra water demand.
- **Missing data:** There are no Senegal- or Sahel-specific planting calendars for okra, partly because it is often multiple-cropped. We do not give a rigid calendar, only note season generalities.

## Eggplant

### Identity and production context

Eggplant (commonly *Solanum melongena* L., also known as **aubergine**, “brinjal”) is grown in Senegal mainly as African eggplant (*Solanum aethiopicum*), known locally as **“garden egg”** or *’beref’*. The common large-fruited eggplant (S. melongena) is less prevalent, though both types may be called “eggplant.” The African types are *widely cultivated from Senegal to Mozambique* and are valued for their high yield and reliability. Eggplants are usually grown in the warm season in garden plots (rainfed or irrigated), and sometimes as a minor crop in rotation. They are seldom grown in protected conditions.

### Soil interpretation

- **Texture and drainage:** Eggplants prefer **well-drained, fertile soils**. They tolerate heavier soils than tomato (due to robust roots), but standing water harms them. Sandy-loam or loamy soils that hold moisture yet drain free water are ideal. In Senegal, the loamy Niayes soils work well; very sandy soils may require extra fertility inputs.
- **Soil pH:** Optimal pH is 6.0–7.0. Slight acidity is acceptable. Very acid soils lead to nutrient deficiencies (especially calcium, causing blossom-end rot), and alkaline soils reduce iron uptake.
- **Organic matter:** Good organic content improves yield. Eggplant benefits from compost/manure – it supports vigorous growth and large fruit clusters. Low-OM soils yield fewer/smaller fruits.
- **N, P, K:** Eggplants are moderate feeders. They need steady N (but not excessive – too much N causes lush foliage and poor fruiting). Phosphorus is needed at transplanting for root establishment; potassium improves fruit quality. Under-fertilized plants set few fruits.
- **CEC:** Higher CEC soils (more clay/OM) store nutrients for eggplant, improving resilience to drought. In coarse-textured soils, use fertigation or regular top-dressing to maintain supply.
- **Salinity & waterlogging:** Eggplant is moderately salt-sensitive; salts reduce fruit set and cause tip burn. Waterlogged soils cause root rot (e.g. *Phytophthora* root rot) and stunted growth. Ensure fields drain fully.

### Climate interpretation

- **Temperature sensitivity:** Eggplant thrives in tropical warmth. Optimal daytime 25–30 °C; night >15 °C. In Senegal’s hot season, eggplant grows rapidly. Daytime exceeding ~35 °C may slow pollination and cause flower drop, but African garden eggs are well adapted to heat. Cold (below 15 °C) slows flowering; rarely an issue.
- **Heat stress:** High heat generally promotes growth, but extreme heat can weaken plants, especially if water-stressed. Shading or mulching can protect plants in extreme sun.
- **Cold stress:** Eggplant is very cold-sensitive. Seedlings or flowers will die at temperatures near 0 °C (irrelevant to Senegal) and growth slows below 15 °C. Thus planting is timed after the coolest months.
- **Humidity and disease:** High humidity can worsen fungal diseases (e.g. powdery mildew on eggplant leaves). Senegal’s mostly dry climate suppresses this except in irrigated dense plantings. Aphid-transmitted viruses can occur in warm, humid conditions. Good spacing and rotation reduce risks.
- **Rainfall/water-stress:** Eggplant needs consistent moisture during flowering and fruiting. Drought causes small, bitter fruits and leaf curling. Conversely, heavy rain can cause wilting and increase pest pressure (e.g. more aphids). Regular watering during dry spells is beneficial.
- **Vulnerable stages:** Flowering and early fruit development need adequate moisture and moderate heat. Stress here aborts buds. Mature fruits can endure short dry spells but will shrivel if prolonged water is lacking.

### Water-management implications

- **Soil type:** On lighter soils, frequent irrigation ensures moisture to depth. On loams, maintain even moisture. In Niayes, careful irrigation yields high-quality large fruits.
- **Drainage:** Eggplant roots need oxygen. Raised beds or ridges are common to avoid pooling. In flat fields, furrow irrigation with drain-off is used.
- **Critical stages:** Maintain good moisture from bloom until fruits reach market size. Reducing water slightly late in ripening can improve dry matter and avoid fruit splitting.
- **Irregular rainfall:** Eggplant can be grown rainfed if rains are adequate (as a staple in some African diets), but yield is erratic. Most market production in Senegal relies on irrigation to avoid dry periods.
- **Irrigation availability:** Drip irrigation is ideal (wet root, dry foliage). Overhead in rainy periods can exacerbate foliar disease.

### Principal risks and mitigations

- **Pests (general):** Eggplant shares pests with tomatoes/pepper. Aphids and whiteflies suck sap and transmit viruses. Flea beetles and cotton stainers feed on leaves/fruit. *Mitigation:* Encourage beneficial insects; use insect-proof netting for seedlings; pick off visible pests. 
- **Diseases:** Fungal diseases (e.g. Fusarium wilt, bacterial wilt) occur in poor drainage or warm soils. Anthracnose or gray mold can rot fruit. *Mitigation:* Plant in well-drained beds; use disease-free seed/seedlings. Rotate to non-solanaceous crops annually.
- **Nutrient limitation:** Low fertility leads to small or few fruits. *Mitigation:* Apply balanced fertilizers or compost. Soil tests guide application; ensure calcium and boron levels to prevent blossom-end rot.
- **Drought:** Okra is said to have a deep root, but eggplant roots are more shallow. Water stress causes blossom drop and bitter, tough fruits. *Mitigation:* Irrigate regularly; mulch to keep soil moist.
- **Waterlogging:** Standing water quickly kills eggplant. *Mitigation:* Avoid irrigation during heavy rains; ensure drainage.
- **Heat:** While tolerant, extreme heat + drought reduces fruit size and plant vigor. *Mitigation:* Provide partial shading (e.g. shade cloth), ensure nutrients and water are ample.
- **Cultural:** African eggplants in Senegal may be left to mature on-the-plant longer (for drying), affecting next cropping if field is continuous. *Mitigation:* Harvest pods (or leaves) earlier for fresh markets to stimulate new growth.

### Evidence limitations

- **Senegal-specific:** There is almost no published data on eggplant in Senegal. The Lost Crops of Africa text highlights African garden egg prevalence, but gives little agronomic detail. Local yield data and best practices are from farmer knowledge, not formal studies.
- **Regional/international:** Much of our agronomy is drawn from global sources on eggplants (both *S. melongena* and African varieties). Specific Senegalese preferences (pod color, market size) are undocumented.
- **Cultivar differences:** African eggplants (smaller, often bitter) have different growth habits than Asian/S. melongena types (larger fruit). Their heat tolerance may differ too. Sources seldom separate these, potentially conflating data.
- **Conflicting data:** Nutrition requirements from temperate climates may not apply; for example, claims that eggplant is “relatively pest-free” stem from observations in Africa, but no local study confirms it. Senegalese fields do suffer pests (as evidenced by pesticide use in Niayes).
- **Missing data:** We lack exact optimal pH or CEC ranges for Senegalese soils; numeric weather thresholds. We give qualitative guidance but stress to use local trials to fine-tune.

## Carrot

### Identity and production context

Carrot (*Daucus carota* L. subsp. *sativus*) is a cool-season root vegetable (local name **“carotte”**). In Senegal, carrots are grown mainly in the **Niayes region** under irrigation during the cooler season. Unlike tropical vegetables, carrots do poorly in heat. They are not a staple crop but are cultivated by market-gardeners for urban markets. Varieties used include Nantes and Chantenay types (somewhat heat-tolerant). Nearly all carrot production in Senegal is irrigated; open-field in fertile loam soils.

### Soil interpretation

- **Texture and drainage:** Carrots need **deep, loose, well-drained soils**. Ideal is sandy loam that allows straight, uniform roots. Even soils prevent forked roots. In Niayes, soils have been tilled deep. Heavy clay soils are unsuitable (roots can’t penetrate and rot easily); very sandy soils dry out and cause stunted roots unless irrigated.
- **Soil pH:** Optimal pH is ~6.0–6.8. Acidic soils (<5.5) limit root growth and carrot can get nutrient disorders. Alkaline soils (pH>7.5) cause iron deficiency chlorosis. Niayes soils are generally in the sweet spot after liming.
- **Organic matter:** Moderate organic matter is beneficial (but too much raw manure can cause root forking). Carrots in organic farming use well-composted manure. Low fertility means smaller roots and poor color; micronutrients (e.g. boron) are critical at lower pH.
- **N, P, K:** Carrots need good phosphorus for root development early, and balanced N (too much leads to excessive top growth and deformed roots). Potassium affects sweetness and root quality. In low-fertility soils, yields are much reduced.
- **CEC:** Higher CEC (more clay/OM) holds nutrients well, helping the long carrot root access them. In sandy soils, nutrients quickly leach, so frequent fertilization is needed.
- **Salinity & waterlogging:** Carrot is **sensitive to salinity**; saline water yields small, bitter roots. Waterlogging is fatal: carrot roots rot under anaerobic conditions. Good drainage is mandatory. Over-irrigation can cause water-lodging; irrigation should wet the soil to depth but allow surface drying between waterings.

### Climate interpretation

- **Temperature sensitivity:** Carrots prefer **cool conditions**. Optimal growing temperature is ~16–21 °C. They germinate best at 15–20°C. Daytime above ~24–27 °C causes root defects (forking, bitterness) and bolting; nights above ~18 °C reduce sweetness. Thus, Senegal’s hot climate (day ~30°C) is not ideal, making carrot largely a winter crop.
- **Heat stress:** High heat accelerates the crop but reduces root quality. In Senegal, carrots are grown in the coolest months (Jan–Apr) or mid-season planting. Even then, afternoon shade or light mulching is used.
- **Cold stress:** Carrots can handle light frost; cool nights improve color and sweetness. Senegal’s winter nights (~15°C) are acceptable and even beneficial (frost is rare except maybe in far north).
- **Humidity and disease:** High humidity favors leaf blight (*Alternaria*) and downy mildew; but Senegal’s dry season limits this. However, if irrigation scheduling causes evening wet foliage, fungus can set in.
- **Rainfall/water-stress:** Carrots need consistent moisture: deficits cause root splitting and reduced size, while excessive water (especially late) can cause root rot. Ideally, maintain even soil moisture through irrigation. Drastic changes (drought then soak) produce deformed roots.

### Water-management implications

- **Soil type:** In deep sandy loams, water penetrates easily, but irrigation must be well-timed. Carrot fields in Niayes often use drip irrigation. In clayey patches, water pools must be avoided.
- **Drainage:** Carrot seedbeds are made on raised beds or ridges to improve drainage. After heavy rain or irrigation, ensure fields dry quickly (tilth helps).
- **Critical stages:** The entire growing period (from germination through root fill) is sensitive. Water stress at any time reduces yield or quality.  
- **Irregular rainfall:** Because carrots are temperate crops, relying on erratic tropical rains is impractical. Farmers sow in the dry season with full irrigation control. If rainfall occurs unexpectedly (rarely late dry season), it cools soils but must be managed to avoid waterlogging.
- **Irrigation volume:** Carrots often receive 400–500 mm over ~4 months. Since rainfall is minimal, nearly all water must come from irrigation.

### Principal risks and mitigations

- **Heat:** Temperatures above ~27–30 °C cause bolting (premature seed stalk), small woody roots, and bitterness. *Mitigation:* Plant in cooler months; use heat-tolerant cultivars if available; provide shade (e.g. interplant with tall crops during hottest weeks).
- **Drought:** Water stress yields stunted, deformed roots. *Mitigation:* Maintain uniform irrigation. Mulch to conserve moisture. Avoid gaps in watering.
- **Nutrient deficiency:** Lean soils produce underdeveloped carrots. *Mitigation:* Apply P-rich starter fertilizer, incorporate compost, and use balanced N (not excessive).
- **Nutrient excess:** Too much N leads to lush tops and split roots. *Mitigation:* Adhere to soil-test recommendations, avoiding N-bomb fertilizers.
- **Root-knot nematodes:** In sandy soils of Niayes, nematodes can cause “forked” carrots. *Mitigation:* Rotate with non-host crops, solarize soil, or use nematode-resistant varieties.
- **Weed competition:** Carrot seedlings are slow, so weeds can outcompete. *Mitigation:* Keep fields well-weeded; shallow hoeing.
- **Disease (blights):** *Alternaria* leaf blight can defoliate crops. *Mitigation:* Rotate crops, use clean seed, and avoid overhead irrigation in humid periods.

### Evidence limitations

- **Senegal-specific:** No formal studies on carrot irrigation or cultivar adaptation in Senegal were found. We base much on temperate data and the assumption carrots are grown only in the coolest season.
- **Regional/international:** Practices (e.g. optimal 16–21°C) are drawn from temperate-region research. Niayes microclimate may warm higher than ideal, challenging these recommendations.
- **Cultivar differences:** Varieties adapted to moderate climates (e.g. Nantes) may underperform in Senegal. Local selections for heat tolerance exist but are undocumented.
- **Conflicting data:** We mention ranges (e.g. temperatures) qualitatively because sources vary. For example, one source says carrots can tolerate 4.5°C, but none of that matters in Senegal’s warmth.
- **Missing data:** No Senegal yield benchmarks were found. All quantitative statements about water, temperature, or yields come from general carrot agronomy.

- Temperate-climate agronomy references (e.g. University extension bulletins).  
- West African market surveys (mention carrot as common Niayes crop).

## Cabbage

### Identity and production context

Cabbage (*Brassica oleracea* var. *capitata*) is a major leafy vegetable in Senegal, especially the **Niayes market-garden zone**. Known locally as **“chou”**, it is eaten cooked in many Senegalese dishes. Cabbage is grown almost entirely under irrigation and frequently under plastic tunnels or simple mini-greenhouses to manage pests and growth. Senegal is the leading supplier of cabbage to regional markets. At project launch (2012), cabbage was the 4th most important horticultural crop nationally (8% of horticultural output). Both local short-cycle hybrids and heirloom types are used, typically planted in the late rains or early dry season and harvested before the peak heat.

### Soil interpretation

- **Texture and drainage:** Cabbage heads form best in loamy soils that hold moisture but also drain well. Soils must be deep to allow root spread. In Senegal, Niayes soils and valley clays that are well-tilled can produce large heads. On heavy clays, risk of waterlogging and nematodes is higher (severe in poorly managed fields). On sands, heads may suffer water stress unless well irrigated.
- **Soil pH:** Optimal pH is ~6.5–7.0. Cabbage tolerates mild acidity (down to ~6.0) but suffers in very low pH with stunted growth. In calcareous soils (higher pH), micronutrients like zinc can become limiting.
- **Organic matter:** High OM leads to bigger heads. In Niayes, farmers often use compost (e.g. slaughterhouse waste compost) to boost yield. Low OM (common in Senegal’s older fields) leads to smaller heads and may require more inorganic fertilizers.
- **N, P, K:** Cabbage is a heavy feeder: needs high N for leaf growth, phosphorus for root and head formation, and potassium for head quality (firmness and storage life). Inadequate N produces small, lacy heads; inadequate P or K reduces head weight and storability.
- **CEC:** Higher CEC soils (higher clay/OM) supply nutrients steadily, which helps hold yields through the season. Low CEC Niayes sands can lose N quickly, requiring split fertilization.
- **Salinity & waterlogging:** Cabbage is *sensitive to salt* – even mild salinity can cause leaf burn and poor head formation. In Niayes, soil salinity (from irrigation or soil type) is managed carefully. Waterlogging also severely affects cabbage: swollen soils cause root rot and hamper growth. Good drainage and controlled irrigation are essential.

### Climate interpretation

- **Temperature sensitivity:** Cabbage prefers cool growing conditions (~15–20 °C). In Senegal, it is grown in the coolest months (Nov–Mar). High temperatures cause premature head splitting and loose leaves. Cabbage head formation slows above ~27 °C.
- **Heat stress:** Hot days (above ~30°C) lead to loose or “puffy” heads and bolting (flowering) of plants. Hence cabbage is not grown in the peak summer. Even midday shade can help during heat spikes. 
- **Cold stress:** Cabbage tolerates light frost (<0°C) which isn’t an issue in Senegal, and night coolness actually aids compact head formation (as in temperate climates). 
- **Humidity and disease:** Cabbage favors moist climates for best growth, but Senegal’s environment forces irrigation. High humidity (either from rain or greenhouse humidity) encourages diseases like downy mildew on leaves. Ventilation in tunnels and careful watering reduce this. Bacterial soft rot can occur in storage if heads are too wet at harvest.
- **Rainfall/water-stress:** Cabbage requires abundant water to form solid heads (soil moisture keeps leaves supple and allows head expansion). Drought during growth leads to small, fibrous heads. In Senegal’s dry season, all water is from irrigation. Too much water at harvest can crack heads.
- **Vulnerable stages:** The rapid leaf-expansion phase (leading up to heading) is most moisture- and nutrient-sensitive. Seedlings and early vegetative plants are robust, but heads forming need constant water.

### Water-management implications

- **Soil type:** On sandy soils, frequent watering is needed to prevent dryness. On heavier soils, avoid over-flooding as cabbage roots are surface-heavy. Drip irrigation with mulch is common in Niayes to keep roots cool and moist.
- **Drainage:** Raised beds help avoid waterlogging. Row orientation and field grading ensure water does not stand.
- **Critical stages:** Full irrigation from transplant until heads near harvest. Reducing water slightly just before harvest can harden heads and improve shelf life.
- **Irregular rainfall:** Cabbage production is timed to avoid rains (growth mainly in dry season); thus rain is not relied on. If unexpected rain occurs, drainage is managed and harvest accelerated.
- **Irrigation availability:** Quality head formation is tied to irrigation. Frequent watering (e.g. 20–30 mm/week) is typical. Since cabbage uses ~80% of its water through leaves, overhead watering is often used outside tunnels, but drip ensures roots stay wet without wetting leaves.

### Principal risks and mitigations

- **Diamondback moth (Plutella xylostella):** This caterpillar is the **most destructive cabbage pest in Senegal**, present year-round in Niayes. It can destroy young leaves and bore into heads. *Mitigation:* Integrated pest management: avoid calendar insecticide spraying (to preserve parasitoids). Use pheromone traps to monitor. Encourage biocontrol; research shows 4 native wasp species parasitize DBM. Rotate with non-brassica crops to break the DBM lifecycle.
- **Cabbage webworm (Hellula undalis):** Another caterpillar pest (found during the STDF project) that chews holes. *Mitigation:* Hand-picking larvae off plants in small fields; use Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) sprays if needed.
- **Nematodes:** Root-knot nematodes (in Niayes sands) can reduce head size and cause mis-shaping. *Mitigation:* Solarization of soil before planting, use of nematode-resistant tomato or melon cover crops in rotation, or nematicide-treated seedlings.
- **Diseases:** Black rot (Xanthomonas campestris) can enter through leaf wounds, causing yellow V-shaped lesions. *Mitigation:* Use disease-free transplants, avoid wounding leaves, and rotate away from brassicas for 2+ years.
- **Heat:** High temperatures can break down glucosinolates and weaken head structure. *Mitigation:* Grow in coolest months; use shade cloth on hottest days if possible.
- **Nutrient deficiency:** Any lack (especially N or K) leads to loose heads. *Mitigation:* Regular fertilization and soil testing. The STDF project improved yields from 15 to 30 t/ha via better inputs and practices.
- **Post-harvest losses:** If heads remain in field too long or are bruised, rot can set quickly. *Mitigation:* Harvest promptly at maturity and handle gently.

### Evidence limitations

- **Senegal-specific:** The STDF/AUMN project provides the most detail on Senegalese cabbage (pests, yields). However, much agronomy (e.g. exact temperature thresholds, fertilizer rates) comes from global cabbage guides.
- **Regional/international:** Information on diseases and pests (DBM, webworm) is Senegalese. Yield goals (30 t/ha) are local after improvements, but may not be reached by resource-poor farmers.
- **Cultivar differences:** Different cabbage varieties vary in cold tolerance and head formation speed. Senegalese farmers use both fast hybrids (2–3 months) and long-season types; these differences aren’t thoroughly documented.
- **Conflicting data:** The agronomy_data.md (if consulted) might contain some older figures (e.g. fertilizer rates); we neither confirmed nor repeated them. Any such outdated claim should be re-checked against modern practice.
- **Missing data:** Post-2014 adoption rates of improved practices are unclear. Also, precise effects of microclimate (Niayes vs. lower rainfall areas) need field data.

- Labou *et al.* (2016) – DBM in Niayes cabbage (Crop Protection).  
- AVRDC/WorldVeg cabbage fact sheets (general).  
- FAO/ISRA extension materials (Senegal).

## Lettuce

### Identity and production context

Lettuce (*Lactuca sativa* L.), locally called **“laitue”**, is a cool-season leafy salad vegetable. In Senegal it is grown intensively in the Niayes horticulture zone during the dry season for local markets. Common types include romaine (*L. sativa var. longifolia*) and iceberg (*L. sativa var. capitata*), with high-market-value baby-leaf mixes. Lettuce is not grown rainfed; it relies on irrigation or plastic tunnels. Niayes’ relatively cooler climate (especially nights) allows quality lettuce heads that are difficult in inland Sahel conditions.

### Soil interpretation

- **Texture and drainage:** Lettuce requires **loose, fertile soils**. Heavy clay causes poor root penetration and slow growth; pure sand leads to excess drainage and nutrient loss. A fine sandy loam (which is found in parts of Niayes) is ideal. Good drainage is essential, especially in winter when rainfall may occur.
- **Soil pH:** Optimal pH is 6.0–7.0. Lettuce tolerates slightly acid soils, but below pH 6 nutrient uptake (e.g. calcium) is impaired, risking tip burn. In Niayes, soils are managed to neutral pH by liming.
- **Organic matter:** Lettuce responds well to organic amendments, which help keep leaf tissue tender. Low organic matter soils produce slower growth. Niayes producers often apply compost (from the local slaughterhouse waste) to achieve high yields and quality.
- **N, P, K:** Lettuce has a short cycle but high nutrient demand (especially N) for rapid leaf growth. Balanced fertilization is crucial. Too little N causes small, yellow leaves; too much N can promote disease and reduce shelf life. Phosphorus and potassium also affect leaf color and solidity.
- **CEC:** Soils with higher CEC retain nutrients for the lettuce during its fast growth. Sandy Niayes soils need split fertilization due to leaching risk.
- **Salinity & waterlogging:** Lettuce is sensitive to salinity; salt uptake can cause leaf burn and tip necrosis. Irrigation water must be low-salt. Waterlogged soil will rot roots quickly (lettuce has a shallow root system). Raised beds help avoid excess moisture.

### Climate interpretation

- **Temperature sensitivity:** Lettuce is a true **cool-season crop**. It grows best at 15–20 °C; above ~24°C it tends to bolt (run to seed) and develop bitter leaves. Senegalese lettuce is therefore grown in winter (Nov–Feb). Even then, sunny days above 28°C can stress plants. 
- **Heat stress:** Lettuce quickly bolts when soil/air temperatures stay above ~25–28°C. Bolting ends the crop (the head goes to seed). Thus summer lettuce is not feasible. Shade cloth or vented tunnels help reduce temperature spikes during hot days.
- **Cold stress:** Lettuce tolerates light frost, but in practice temperatures below ~4–5°C slow growth. Senegal’s Niayes rarely drop that low in winter.
- **Humidity and disease:** Lettuce thrives in a humid atmosphere (leaf cooling) but high humidity on leaves can lead to fungal diseases (e.g. *Botrytis* gray mold). In Senegal’s dry-season production, humidity is only from dew or irrigation; growers ventilate tunnels to prevent mildew.
- **Rainfall/water-stress:** Lettuce needs constant moisture. Drought causes small heads and bitter leaves. However, excessive water at maturity can split heads and invite rot. Scheduling irrigation in mild amounts daily or every-other-day is common.

### Water-management implications

- **Soil type:** In well-drained Niayes soils, frequent light irrigation maintains soil moisture evenly. In less porous areas, care is taken not to pond water.
- **Drainage:** Raised beds are universal for lettuce to avoid stagnation. Fields are flat but furrowed or bedded.
- **Critical stages:** Vegetative growth and head formation need moisture. Lettuce does not enter a “rest” phase – water stress at any time reduces head weight.
- **Irregular rainfall:** Lettuce fields often have covers during unexpected rains to prevent crop damage. Since production is timed for the dry season, rain is not relied upon; irrigation is primary.
- **Irrigation availability:** Drip or overhead sprinklers can be used. In greenhouses, misting may be used to raise humidity without puddling. Typically, lettuce requires >20 mm per week in arid conditions.

### Principal risks and mitigations

- **Heat/bolting:** Temperatures above ~25°C cause lettuce to bolt and turn bitter. *Mitigation:* Grow only in winter; use light-filtering cloth or shade nets on hot days; irrigate in early morning to cool soil.
- **Water stress:** Even brief drought kills lettuce quality. *Mitigation:* Irrigate frequently; use mulch or overhead fogging to maintain humidity.
- **Nutrient imbalance:** Fast growth demands adequate NPK. *Mitigation:* Use frequent, small fertilizer doses (e.g. fertigate in drip) to match the quick cycle. Soil testing and topdressing are practiced.
- **Pests:** Aphids and thrips can infest lettuce, deforming leaves. Snails/slugs can be severe if close to water. *Mitigation:* Biological controls (lacewings, predatory mites) are encouraged. Barriers or traps for snails; handpicking is common.
- **Diseases:** Gray mold (*Botrytis*) and downy mildew thrive on cool, moist leaves. *Mitigation:* Keep leaves dry before harvest, ventilate tunnels, and practice crop rotation (no consecutive lettuce on the same land).
- **Salinity:** Irrigation with brackish water causes tip burn. *Mitigation:* Monitor water quality; flush soil periodically to remove salts.

### Evidence limitations

- **Senegal-specific:** No detailed studies on lettuce in Senegal were found. We rely on general cool-season crop guidelines. However, Niayes is a unique microclimate; exact temperature and humidity data for best growth there are lacking.
- **Regional/international:** Lettuce info largely comes from Europe/North America (ideal 15–20°C) and might not account for tropical sunshine intensity. We assume similar physiology applies.
- **Cultivar differences:** Looseleaf vs. headed varieties have different tolerances (looseleaf bolts faster but grows quicker). Senegal likely uses bolt-resistant hybrids, but specifics are not documented.
- **Conflicting data:** None of our sources gave contradictory guidance. The main uncertainty is how close Senegalese practice is to temperate norms.
- **Missing data:** No data on typical yields or water use in Senegal. Market demands (e.g. leaf type, color) are known to growers but not in literature. We do not present such info.

### Sources

- ISHS Niayes survey (not specific to lettuce, but Niayes context).  
- Extension sources (U.S./Europe) on lettuce temperate requirements.  
- FAO/WorldVeg manuals (leafy vegetable production).

## Watermelon

### Identity and production context

Watermelon (*Citrullus lanatus* [Thunb.] Matsum. & Nakai), including both sweet dessert types and egusi (seed) melons, is an important fruit crop in Senegal. Senegal is the **largest tropical African producer** of watermelon (224,000 t annually). Much of it is grown in river valleys and irrigated fields, though some dryland cultivation occurs after rains. Both common dessert watermelons (large, sweet, fleshy fruit) and seed melons (*C. lanatus var. mucosospermus*, called “egusi” or *beref* in Wolof) are cultivated. Watermelons are typically planted in late dry season under irrigation so fruits mature in late dry season and early rainy season.

### Soil interpretation

- **Texture and drainage:** Watermelon thrives on **well-drained sandy loam soils**. Its shallow feeder roots are easily drowned in clayey or impermeable soils. In Niayes and river valleys, fields are prepared with deep sandy loams. Heavy soils (clay) cause vine stress and fruit defects. Sandy soils warm quickly, favoring germination, but must be rich in organic matter to hold moisture.
- **Soil pH:** Optimal pH is 6.0–7.0. Mild acidity is tolerated, but extremes cause nutrient issues. Tropical African watermelons have adapted to a range, but Senegalese growers target neutral pH via lime if needed.
- **Organic matter:** Watermelons consume and conserve lots of water and nutrients. High organic matter supports vigorous vine growth. Compost applications (e.g. Niayes compost programs) can be beneficial, improving moisture retention and feeding the long vine cycle.
- **N, P, K:** Watermelon needs high K for sweetness and color. Nitrogen is important early for vine growth, but excessive N late can lead to too much foliage and less sweet fruit. Phosphorus at planting helps root establishment and fruit set. Deficiencies lower yield and sugar content.
- **CEC:** Higher CEC soils store more nutrients, which is important given watermelon’s long season (~4-5 months). In low CEC soils, use split fertilization or fertigation to supply K and N over time.
- **Salinity & waterlogging:** Watermelon is *moderately salt-sensitive*: salinity reduces fruit size and sugar. Senegal’s saline coastal soils require selecting less-sensitive varieties or leaching salts. Waterlogging quickly kills vines and spoils fruits. Slight excess moisture can cause fruit splitting or increased rot; drainage is critical.

### Climate interpretation

- **Temperature sensitivity:** Watermelon is a hot-season crop. Seeds germinate and vines grow best at 25–35 °C. Nights warmer than ~18 °C allow continuous growth. In Senegal, the hot season (especially March–May, ~30–40 °C days) is ideal for melon development. Cooler weather slows fruit coloring and sugar accumulation.
- **Heat stress:** Very high temperatures (above ~40 °C) can cause sunscald on exposed fruits and leaf wilt, though mature vines tolerate heat well if well-watered. The greatest risk is midday heat in young transplants; farmers often use shade or watered trenches to cool plants at this stage.
- **Cold stress:** Watermelon will not set fruit if nights dip below ~12–15 °C. There is no cold in Senegal’s growing season, so this is not a concern. Seeds won’t germinate if soil stays below 20°C, but Senegal’s soils warm adequately in the dry season.
- **Humidity and disease:** High humidity promotes fungal diseases (anthracnose, downy mildew) on leaves and rot on fruit. Senegal’s dry air reduces these, but if fields are heavily irrigated or if rainy season rains occur before harvest, diseases can damage vines and fruits. Monitoring and fungicide (organic or minimal-risk) are sometimes used in humid microclimates.
- **Rainfall/water-stress:** Watermelon vines have high water demand during fruit enlargement. Drought at fruit filling leads to small, white seeds and pale flesh. However, too much water just before harvest dilutes sugars and can cause fruit cracking. Ideally, vines get ample water until a few weeks pre-harvest, then irrigation is reduced.
- **Vulnerable stages:** The plant is most sensitive to water stress during flowering and early fruit set (yield is determined by fruit set) and later during fruit filling (size and sweetness). Seedling stage also needs consistent moisture until vines establish.

### Water-management implications

- **Soil type:** Sandy soils warm fast and drain well, but need frequent irrigation. On heavier soils, ensure beds are raised to prevent saturating roots. Watermelons planted on mounds can tolerate heavier rains.
- **Drainage:** Avoid any standing water. Raised beds or ridges are standard. After rains, fields should dry out for vine health.
- **Critical stages:** Adequate water from bloom through fruit filling is essential. Vines cover ground and shade soil, so evaporation is high; ensure irrigation keeps up.
- **Irregular rainfall:** In Senegal’s mainly dry season cultivation, farmers irrigate from wells or canals. Rain is usually unwelcome until harvest; unexpected rains are managed by adjusting water scheduling.
- **Irrigation vs rainfall:** Watermelon water demand is often >500 mm over its season. In Niayes/Senegal River irrigated fields, drip or flood irrigation is applied regularly. Without irrigation, yields are very low.

### Principal risks and mitigations

- **Drought:** Water stress causes reduced sweetness and smaller fruits. *Mitigation:* Ensure consistent irrigation. Use water-conserving practices (mulch, maintain vine cover). In drought years, plant varieties with shorter maturity to avoid the driest months.
- **Heat extremes:** Sunscald can occur on exposed fruit. *Mitigation:* Keep vines healthy (so foliage covers fruit) and use shading nets during blistering sun if needed. Drip irrigation to avoid leaf wetting and mold.
- **Disease (anthracnose, gummy stem blight):** If humidity rises, foliage/fruit spots can occur. *Mitigation:* Crop rotation, removing old vines. Fungicide sprays may be used sparingly. Harvest promptly when ripe.
- **Pests:** Aphids and whiteflies can transmit viruses (e.g. mosaic). Adult cucumber beetles (rare in Senegal) chew leaves. *Mitigation:* Use row covers on seedlings, monitor and control with biocontrols, insecticidal soaps if needed.
- **Nutrient deficiency:** Poor soils lead to little vine growth and pale, tasteless fruits. *Mitigation:* Fertilize thoroughly pre-plant (especially K). Use compost.
- **Over-irrigation:** Too much water at fruit ripening splits melons. *Mitigation:* Reduce watering 2–3 weeks before harvest (“drying out” period). Drain fields if heavy rain threatens.
- **Harvest losses:** Overripe melons crack or rot. *Mitigation:* Harvest at peak ripeness (color change, field sound). Avoid bruising during picking and transport.  

### Evidence limitations

- **Senegal-specific:** PROTA reports Senegal watermelon output but gives no agronomic detail. Local cultivar adaptations (e.g. Niayes-adapted hybrids) are not documented publicly.
- **Regional/international:** Soil and climate guidance comes from tropical watermelon literature. For instance, the *only* West African data we found was production tonnages, so agronomic rules are assumed from similar hot climates.
- **Cultivar differences:** Dessert vs. egusi (seed melon) have different uses; egusi types (C. lanatus var. mucosospermus) need higher rainfall (700–1000 mm annually) because seeds develop. We focus on fruit types; seed melons may have distinct needs not captured here.
- **Conflicting data:** Some sources give broad rainfall ranges for watermelon. We have not tried to reconcile these beyond common-sense ranges (none is contradicted by our narrative).
- **Missing data:** Senegal’s actual on-farm irrigation schedules, planting densities, and season lengths would refine this advice but are not available.

## Bell Pepper

### Identity and production context

Bell pepper (sweet pepper; *Capsicum annuum* L. var. *grossum*) is a non-spicy pepper grown for its fleshy fruits, called “poivron” or *piment doux*. In Senegal it is grown on a small scale by market gardeners, often alongside tomatoes and chilis. Demand exists for green and red bell peppers in urban markets. Cultivation practices and conditions largely mirror those of chili peppers (same species, similar growing season). There is little large-scale research on bell peppers in Senegal; we rely on general capsicum agronomy.

### Soil interpretation

- **Texture and drainage:** Like other peppers, bell peppers prefer **loamy, fertile, well-drained soils**. Heavy, waterlogged soils cause root rot and poor fruit set. Loams with good tilth in Niayes are ideal. On light sandy soils, careful irrigation is needed.
- **Soil pH:** Ideal pH 6.0–6.8. Moderate acidity is tolerated; strongly acidic soils (<5.5) lead to stunted growth. Senegalese pepper plots are usually kept near neutral by liming.
- **Organic matter:** Bell peppers need organic matter for steady nutrient supply. Low fertility soils produce few or small fruits. Compost/manure improves soil structure and feeding.
- **N, P, K:** High fertilizer demand. Nitrogen encourages leafiness (important for canopy) but excessive late-season N delays fruit maturity. Phosphorus is important for root health and flowering, potassium for fruit size and color. In tropical soils, ensure balanced fertilization – peppers will grow poorly on nutrient-depleted Niayes soils without inputs.
- **CEC:** Higher CEC soils hold nutrients (especially K) that peppers need over their several-month growth. In low-CEC sands, use split applications or fertigation to provide N and K through the season.
- **Salinity & waterlogging:** Bell peppers are moderately sensitive to salinity; some varieties (like chilis) can tolerate slightly more, but salts generally reduce fruit set and size. Waterlogging is harmful: roots need oxygen, and wet conditions predispose pepper to fungal diseases. Ensure free drainage in fields.

### Climate interpretation

- **Temperature sensitivity:** Bell peppers grow best around 22–28 °C. Compared to chili peppers, sweet bells are slightly more sensitive to cool temperatures (they are tropical but are often grown in warmer seasons). Night temperatures below ~15 °C slow growth; days above ~32 °C cause blossom drop and poor fruit set. Thus, in Senegal peppers are planted in late dry season to mature just before the hottest period.
- **Heat stress:** High heat (>32°C) reduces yield: flowers drop, fruits remain small, and pigments (red color) may fail to develop fully. Plants often wilt during midday. *Mitigation:* Adequate irrigation, mulching, or shade nets are helpful.
- **Cold stress:** Peppers are killed by frost. At lowest Senegal winter temperatures (~15°C), growth is slow but they still produce. Quick transplanting after the coolest weeks is practiced.
- **Humidity and disease:** High humidity promotes black leaf spot, anthracnose, and bacterial leaf blight. Senegal’s irrigation practices aim to wet soil, not foliage, to minimize this. Excessive canopy humidity (in tunnels) can cause blossom rot. Ventilation in tunnels is key.
- **Rainfall/water-stress:** Bell peppers need even moisture. Drought causes small, deformed fruits and orange/yellow streaks. Overwatering near harvest causes fruit splitting. Typically, drip irrigation is scheduled for several times per week through fruit set.

### Water-management implications

- **Soil type:** In sandy soils, peppers require more frequent watering. On heavy soils, avoid puddling. Mulching is common to reduce evaporation and heat stress.
- **Drainage:** Ensure soils do not stay soggy. Raised beds and careful water application prevent root suffocation.
- **Critical stages:** Flowering and initial fruit swelling are most water-sensitive. Irrigation should be heavier at these times. Mature fruits can tolerate slightly less frequent watering.
- **Irregular rainfall:** Peppers do not rely on rainfall; farmers use irrigation based on soil moisture. Unseasonal heavy rains before harvest can degrade fruit (softening, splitting).
- **Irrigation availability:** Bell pepper production in Senegal is limited by water availability. Niayes growers usually have irrigation pumps/wells; lack of irrigation generally precludes profitable pepper cultivation.

### Principal risks and mitigations

- **Heat stress:** Unlike chili (which can set fruit under higher heat), bell peppers often abort flowers above ~32°C. *Mitigation:* Use heat-tolerant varieties if available; ensure full irrigation; use mulch or shade. Plant early so that fruiting occurs before the hottest months.
- **Water stress:** Causes flower drop and misshapen fruits. *Mitigation:* Drip irrigate regularly; monitor soil moisture. Do not let soil dry out during flowering.
- **Nutrient imbalance:** Deficient soils yield poor-quality fruit. *Mitigation:* Apply balanced NPK; peppers are heavy feeders. Organic matter (compost) helps sustain nutrients.
- **Pests:** Aphids, spider mites, and thrips attack bell pepper. Spider mites are especially problematic in hot, dry conditions (they suck leaf juices, causing speckling). *Mitigation:* Use miticides or predatory mites if needed; frequent overhead watering can reduce mites (they dislike water). Aphids can be washed off or controlled with insecticidal soaps.
- **Diseases:** Blossom end rot (calcium deficiency) causes dark cavities on bottoms of fruits. *Mitigation:* Maintain even soil moisture (fluctuations cause Ca transport issues) and ensure adequate calcium (lime soils if needed). Also prevent foliage wetness to avoid blights.
- **Harvest damage:** Bell peppers bruise easily. *Mitigation:* Handle gently; harvest with a bit of stem to avoid tearing. Keep harvested fruits cool and shaded.

### Evidence limitations

- **Senegal-specific:** There is very little specific to sweet pepper vs. hot pepper. We assume similarities with chili pepper requirements, but sweet bell pepper may require a slightly cooler climate (not well documented).
- **Regional/international:** Much pepper agronomy comes from Asia and Americas. Bell pepper in Senegal may have unique climate responses (e.g. midday heat vs. humidity).
- **Cultivar differences:** Market-bought bell pepper cultivars (often short-day) may underperform in Senegal. Some “bell pepper” production might actually be semi-hot types. We do not have variety-specific data.
- **Conflicting data:** Not applicable beyond temperature thresholds (no reliable sources disagree, we use ranges). 
- **Missing data:** No Senegalese variety trials or water use studies for bell peppers were found. Our advice is inferred from chili pepper data and general capsaicin/vegetable guides.

## Cross-crop evidence gaps

Reviewing these crops reveals common information gaps requiring field validation in Senegal:

- **Temperature and rainfall thresholds:** Many values (e.g. optimal germination/fruit set temperatures, crop water needs) are drawn from general vegetable science. Senegal-specific crop coefficients and phenology data would improve irrigation scheduling and crop choice.  
- **Soil and cultivar interactions:** Numeric soil thresholds (e.g. exact pH and CEC ranges for good yield) from crops_merged.json were not independently verified by citations and often lack local calibration. Farmer-managed field trials are needed to test recommended soil limits (e.g. how low pH, how much sand, how high salinity can crops tolerate in Senegal conditions).  
- **Variety performance:** There is little documented difference among local cultivars. For example, “salt-tolerant tomatoes” or “heat-tolerant carrot varieties” exist in seed catalogs, but their relative advantage in Senegal’s soils and climate is not quantified. Breeding trials in Senegal would clarify genetic differences.  
- **Pests and diseases:** Many pests (e.g. African bean mosaic virus on okra, fusarium on eggplant) have not been studied in Senegal specifically. We rely on generic insect/disease lists. Local surveys and monitoring (beyond Niayes) could identify which threats are primary vs. incidental.  
- **Soil fertility management:** Recommended fertilization (rates, timing) was not Senegal-specific. Field experiments to optimize N-P-K and organic amendments under Niayes vs. other zones would help. Similarly, local composting practices (like Niayes slaughterhouse compost) need quantification for nutrient content.  
- **Water management:** Data on actual evapotranspiration (ETc) per crop and season in Senegal is limited. Studies using Lysimeters or sensors in Niayes for each crop would improve irrigation guidance beyond “rainfall vs. irrigation volumes.”  
- **Protected cultivation:** Greenhouse/shade cloth use is growing but understudied. How much do these structures extend the season or improve yields for each crop in Senegal’s light/temperature? Local trials of net houses could fill this gap.  
- **Post-harvest:** Crop post-harvest losses (e.g. bolting rates for lettuce at given temperatures, shelf life of local varieties) are undocumented. Surveys of market quality and storage practices would complement field data.  

In summary, while basic agronomic principles can be applied to Senegal’s context, many specifics remain to be validated by local research and field experience.
