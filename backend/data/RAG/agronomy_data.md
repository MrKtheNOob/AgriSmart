# Strategic Analysis of the Senegalese Agricultural Ecosystem: Productivity, Pedological Resilience, and Food Sovereignty (2024-2034)

The agricultural sector in Senegal is currently positioned at a transformative juncture, transitioning from a historical reliance on rainfed subsistence and colonial-era cash crops toward a multi-sectoral framework defined by the pursuit of absolute food sovereignty. This transition is codified in the National Agricultural Transformation Plan and the recently unveiled Food Sovereignty Strategy (2025-2034), which together represent a comprehensive attempt to restructure the agrarian economy of the country.

As of 2025, the sector remains the backbone of the Senegalese social fabric, employing approximately 60% of the active population and contributing nearly 17% to the national Gross Domestic Product (GDP). However, the sector faces a complex matrix of structural vulnerabilities, including acute climate variability, chronic soil degradation, and a historical dependency on food imports, particularly rice and wheat.

---

## 1. The Institutional Framework of Agricultural Innovation

Central to the resilience of the Senegalese agricultural system is the Institut Sénégalais de Recherches Agricoles (ISRA). Founded in 1974 as the successor to colonial-era research organizations such as the Institut de Recherches pour les Huiles et Oléagineux (IRHO), the Centre Technique Forestier Tropical (CTFT), and the Institut de Recherches Agronomiques Tropicales et des Cultures Vivrières (IRAT), ISRA recently marked its 50-year anniversary in November 2024. This milestone underscored the institute's role in shifting the national agricultural paradigm from simple "food security" to the more robust concept of "food sovereignty."

Throughout its five-decade history, ISRA has registered 232 crop varieties, each tailored to the specific pedo-climatic conditions of Senegal's diverse ecological zones—ranging from the arid North to the humid Casamance. In 2025, the institution's research agenda is increasingly focused on agroecology as a sustainable alternative to the conventional model based on synthetic chemical inputs. This shift is exemplified by the introduction of the "Asdia" banana variety, which is viewed as a turning point for family farming due to its resilience against the climate-induced stressors that have historically hampered the banana value chain.

The current strategic deployment of ISRA, under the leadership of experts such as Dr. Moustapha Gueye, emphasizes the reconstruction of national seed capital. The Seed Production Unit for Cereals and Legumes (UPSem/CL) is at the forefront of this effort, aiming to ensure that the country can meet its own requirements for high-quality, climate-adapted seeds for staples like rice, groundnuts, millet, maize, and cowpeas. This effort is bolstered by the 50x2030 initiative and cooperation with international bodies like the FAO, which have focused on improving the collection and dissemination of agricultural microdata through the Directorate of Analysis, Forecasting and Agricultural Statistics (DAPSA).

## 2. The 2024-2025 Campaign: Statistical Realignment and Production Dynamics

The 2024-2025 agricultural campaign has been defined by a significant divergence in performance across different value chains and a fundamental "rebasing" of official statistics. While certain sectors, like groundnut production, have shown record-breaking performance, others have faced declines that are as much a result of climatic reality as they are of improved data accuracy.

### Groundnut and Cereal Performance
Senegal has historically been a global leader in groundnut production, and the 2024-2025 campaign is expected to cross the threshold of 900,000 tonnes of groundnuts. This achievement is the result of favorable rainfall, the increased availability of inputs, and a reinforced organization of producers within the framework of the National Agricultural Transformation Plan.

In contrast, provisional results for the same campaign show a decline in cereal production. The production of rice and fonio decreased by 10.7% and 33.1%, respectively. Rice production, for instance, was evaluated at 946,209 tonnes, compared to 1,059,461 tonnes in the previous campaign. 

However, it is critical to interpret these figures through the lens of the April 2025 statistical reform. The Senegalese government revamped its official production data methodology, leading to a significant downward revision of historical production numbers to create a more reliable baseline for future planning. For instance, the MY 2024/25 milled rice production was re-estimated at 600,000 MT, reflecting the new, more stringent criteria for assessing yields and harvested areas.

### Estimated Agricultural Output and Variation (2024-2025)

| Crop Speculation | Previous Cycle (T) | 2024-2025 Provisional (T) | Variation (%) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Groundnuts (Arachide) | ~800,000 | > 900,000 | +12.5% |

The performance of rainfed cereals has been hampered by localized flooding in the Senegal, Gambia, and Faleme river basins, which resulted in the loss of approximately 2,800 hectares of rice alone. Despite these losses, the government has moved to stabilize the sector through a 120 billion FCFA allocation for the campaign, which included a 22% reduction in the price of fertilizers to help offset the rising costs of production.

## 3. Pedological Characterization: Soil Resilience and Management

The success of any agricultural strategy in Senegal depends on a nuanced understanding of its soil systems. The country is dominated by three main categories of soil: Dior, Deck, and Deck-Dior, each of which presents unique challenges and opportunities for cultivation.

### Dior Soils (Sandy Ferruginous)
Dior soils, which cover roughly 20% of the national territory and 70% of the Diourbel region, are characterized by an extremely high sand content, often exceeding 95%. These soils are found on ancient dunes (the Cayor erg) and are naturally poor in organic matter and nutrients. Surface layers typically contain less than 0.2% organic carbon and 0.19% nitrogen.

In the traditional "Peanut Basin," Dior soils are managed through two distinct spatial systems:
* **Champs de Case (Toll Keur):** Fields located adjacent to residences that receive regular applications of animal manure and household waste. These areas are characterized by higher fertility and are primarily used for continuous millet production.
* **Champs de Brousse (Bush Fields):** Fields located further from villages where fallowing has largely disappeared. These fields are used for millet-groundnut rotations and rely heavily on the nitrogen-fixing properties of the *Faidherbia albida* (Kadd) tree.

### Deck and Deck-Dior Soils (Hydromorphic)
Deck soils are hydromorphic and are typically found in topographical depressions where rainwater accumulates. They have a higher clay and silt content (10% to 15%) and are more structured than Dior soils. These soils are naturally richer, with organic carbon levels between 0.5% and 0.8%, making them suitable for more intensive cereal production and certain horticultural crops.

Deck-Dior soils represent a transitional phase, combining the permeability of sand with the fertility and water retention of clay-rich deeper horizons.

Historical data from Ndiamsil shows that between 1967 and 1999, assimilable phosphorus levels in bush fields dropped from $30\text{ ppm}$ to $11\text{ ppm}$, a trend that continues to pressure yields in the absence of subsidized mineral fertilizers.

## 4. The Cereal Value Chain: Rice, Millet, and Maize

The cereal sector is the pillar of the Senegalese diet, yet it remains the most vulnerable to climate shocks. The state's goal of achieving rice self-sufficiency by 2017 was not met, leading to the current revised strategy that targets 2030 and beyond.

### Rice Production Systems
Rice cultivation in Senegal is split between the intensive, irrigated systems of the Senegal River Valley (SRV) and the traditional, rainfed systems of the Casamance and the Southern regions.
* **Senegal River Valley (SRV):** This region yields between 4 tonnes per hectare during the dry season and 6 to 10 tonnes of paddy during the rainy season. High yields are supported by modern hydraulic equipment and the presence of the Société Nationale d'Aménagement et d'Exploitation des Terres du Delta (SAED).
* **Casamance and South:** Yields are much lower, typically ranging from 2.0 to 3.5 tonnes per hectare. These systems are often managed by women and are highly susceptible to saltwater intrusion in mangrove areas.

The economic structure of rice production is heavily influenced by state intervention. The farm-gate price for paddy rice is currently set at $160\text{ FCFA/kg}$ ($\$0.28\text{/kg}$), which includes a base price of $130\text{ FCFA}$ and a $30\text{ FCFA}$ subsidy paid by the government. Despite this support, the cost of hydraulic management (redevance) remains a burden for many producers, with redevances to the OMVS and SAED evaluated at approximately $41,000\text{ FCFA}$ per hectare for irrigated perimeters.

### Millet and Maize
Millet (*Pennisetum americanum*) is the most important cereal by area, occupying 72% of cereal-cropped land. Two main varieties are cultivated:
* **Souna:** A precocious variety (80 to 100 days) adapted to the less rainy zones of the North and Center.
* **Sanio:** A tardive variety (130 to 150 days) restricted to the more humid South and East.

Maize is primarily used for the animal feed industry and has seen an increase in cultivation area due to its high yield potential (4 to $5\text{ t/ha}$) under controlled conditions. ISRA has developed several maize varieties, such as the "Composite" and "Intermediate" types, which are resistant to diseases like Helminthosporiose and Rust.

## 5. Horticulture: The Economic Engine of the Niayes and the North

Horticulture has emerged as the most dynamic sub-sector of Senegalese agriculture, with production growing by 60% between 2012 and 2018. The sector is driven by urban demand and export opportunities, with onions and tomatoes leading the way.

### Onion Production and Economic Viability
The onion sector is a strategic priority, with a goal of achieving self-sufficiency and reducing the substantial import volumes during the off-season. In the Senegal River Valley, onion yields reach 30 to $32\text{ t/ha}$. The technical itinerary for a single hectare of onions involves significant capital, with total campaign charges estimated at $1,520,800\text{ FCFA}$.

This high profitability (near 200% margin) is conditional on the producer's ability to time the market. Between March and May, a period of peak availability, prices can drop significantly, whereas early or late harvests can command much higher prices.

### Tomato Cultivation: Industrial vs. Fresh Market
Tomato production is divided into industrial tomatoes for processing (mostly in the North) and fresh market tomatoes (mostly in the Niayes).
* **Greenhouse / Shelter:** Round tomato yields can reach $150\text{ t/ha}$ under "Canarian net" shelters in the St. Louis region.
* **Field / Hivernage:** Yields are lower, around 30 to $60\text{ t/ha}$ during the cool season and 10 to $25\text{ t/ha}$ during the rainy season. Technical requirements for tomatoes are stringent: a soil pH of 5.8 to 6.2 is optimal, and the crop is highly sensitive to salinity ($EC > 2\text{ mS/cm}$). Water requirements are approximately $5,000\text{ m}^3\text{/ha}$ for a 130-day cycle.

## 6. The Mango Sector: Export Potential and Post-Harvest Realities

Mangoes are Senegal's leading fruit crop, accounting for 60% of national fruit production. The sector has seen substantial export growth, reaching 24,581 tonnes in 2021, worth 11 billion FCFA. The Casamance region alone contributes between 60% and 75% of the national mango output.

### Export Dynamics and Market Access
Despite high production volumes (around 130,000 tonnes annually), only a fraction is exported. The primary variety for export is the "Kent" mango, valued for its flavor and shelf life. However, the sector is plagued by post-harvest losses, which can reach up to 80% due to fruit fly infestations and a lack of cold chain infrastructure.

To address these losses, there is an increasing focus on the transformation of mangoes into juice, dried slices, and jam. However, microbiological studies of sliced mangoes sold in Dakar show high levels of *Escherichia coli* and other contaminants, highlighting the need for improved hygiene in the transformation process.

## 7. Climatic Challenges and Agronomic Adaptation

Senegal's agriculture is intrinsically linked to the movement of the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), which dictates the onset and offset of the rainy season (Hivernage).

### The Agronomic Onset of the Rainy Season
The onset of the rainy season is the most critical variable for rainfed crops. Farmers often rely on a "rule of thumb"—planting after two days of rain—but this is highly risky. The scientific definition of a "successful" onset involves at least $20\text{ mm}$ of rain over 3 consecutive days, without a dry spell of more than 10 to 20 days in the following month. Failure to time the planting correctly leads to "seedling death," a common occurrence in the Northern and Central zones where the rainy season is shorter (approx. 45 days) compared to the South (150 days).

### Groundwater Depletion in the Niayes
In the Niayes region, the intensive horticultural system is dependent on the quaternary sand aquifer. Research indicates that the current recharge from rainfall is insufficient to cover the withdrawals for irrigation. This has led to a declining water table, increasing the "lift" or pumping distance for farmers and thereby increasing the energy cost of production. As a result, farmers are shifting away from water-intensive, low-profitability crops like carrots toward more high-value, resilient options.

## 8. Macro-Economic Policy: Input Subsidies and Incentives

The Government of Senegal's 2024-2025 campaign is supported by an interventionist policy on input prices. The goal is to lower the barrier to entry for modern agricultural practices for smallholders.

The allocation of inputs has also taken a social dimension, with a directive to reserve 20% of input quotas for women farmers and to distribute seeds for free in areas affected by flooding. Despite these subsidies, the dependency on imported mineral fertilizers remains a point of vulnerability, particularly given the global price volatility of $P_2O_5$ and Urea.

## 9. Future Outlook: The Path to 2034

The Senegalese agricultural sector is moving toward a model of "Competitive Sovereignty." The Food Sovereignty Strategy (2025-2034) emphasizes several key pillars:

1.  **Reconstruction of Seed Capital:** Ensuring domestic production of certified, high-yield seeds to reduce reliance on informal seed exchanges.
2.  **Irrigation Expansion:** Increasing the irrigated area from the current 31,234 hectares to mitigate the impact of erratic rainfall.
3.  **Agroecology and Soil Health:** Promoting the restoration of Dior and Deck soils through organic amendments and agroforestry systems involving *Faidherbia albida*.
4.  **Value Chain Integration:** Increasing the rate of industrial transformation for fruits and vegetables to reduce post-harvest losses and capture more value in the domestic and regional markets.

In summary, the 2024-2025 campaign serves as a diagnostic baseline for this decade-long strategy. While immediate challenges like localized flooding and statistical revisions may suggest a contraction, the underlying institutional strength of ISRA and the significant fiscal commitments to input reduction suggest a trajectory toward greater resilience and self-sufficiency. The long-term success of this transformation will depend on the sector's ability to balance intensive production with the ecological preservation of the country's fragile soil and water resources.
